Most focus advice is written for people who are already fairly focused.
Block your calendar, silence notifications, work in ninety-minute
sprints — it all assumes a mind that will happily settle once the
distractions are removed. If that is your mind, this post is not for you,
and I am a little jealous.

For the rest of us, distraction is not an interruption to concentration.
It is the resting state. The mind wanders not because the phone buzzed
but because wandering is what it does, and the phone is just the nearest
exit. Remove the phone and it finds another door. So the goal cannot be a
distraction-free environment. The goal is to do good work anyway, inside
a mind that will not sit still.

## Stop fighting the wandering

The first thing that helped was giving up on the fantasy of sustained,
effortless focus. I do not have thirty clean minutes in me. I have maybe
seven, then a pull toward something else, then — if I handle it right —
another seven.

The trick is what happens at minute seven. The instinct is to obey the
pull: check the thing, open the tab, "just quickly" look. That is the
move that ends the session, because the tab leads to another tab and
twenty minutes later you are somewhere else entirely. The alternative is
to notice the pull and stay in the seat for thirty more seconds. Not
thirty more minutes — that is too much to ask. Just thirty seconds past
the urge. Often that is enough for the next seven minutes to start.

## A smaller unit than the task

Distracted minds choke on big tasks. "Write the report" is not a thing my
attention can hold; it is a cloud, and I bounce off it toward email. What
my attention can hold is "write the first ugly sentence of the report."

So I shrink the unit until it is almost insultingly small. Not the
chapter, the paragraph. Not the paragraph, the sentence. The rule is that
the next action has to be small enough that starting it is easier than
avoiding it. A distracted mind will not climb a wall, but it will step
over a curb, and enough curbs get you up the hill.

This is also why I keep a scrap of paper next to me. When a thought pulls
at me — a thing to buy, an email to send, a doubt — I write it down
instead of chasing it. The paper is a promise that I will deal with it
later, and that promise is usually enough to let the thought go. Half of
what looks like distraction is just the fear of forgetting something.

## Build the environment for the bad version of you

The calm-mind advice says: remove distractions. The distracted-mind
version is stricter, because I know I will cheat. So I make cheating
expensive. The phone goes in another room, not face-down on the desk —
face-down is a two-second reach and I will take it. The browser opens to
a blank page, not to whatever I last left open. Friction in the right
places is worth more than willpower, because willpower is exactly the
thing I am short of.

## What deep work actually feels like here

It is not a serene flow state. It is a series of small returns —
wandering, noticing, coming back, wandering again, coming back again.
On a good day the returns get quicker and the stretches get longer, and
somewhere in there real work gets done. On a bad day I just keep coming
back, and coming back is the whole skill.

If your mind is quiet, focus is a scheduling problem. If your mind is
loud, focus is a returning problem. Solve the one you actually have. The
work does not care whether you got there in one clean stretch or forty
small ones — it only cares that you kept sitting back down.
