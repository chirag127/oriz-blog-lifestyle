For a while I collected morning routines the way other people collect
apps. Cold plunge, journaling, twenty minutes of meditation, a green
drink, a workout, a reading block — all before the sun was properly up.
I lasted about nine days each time. The problem was never discipline.
The problem was that the routine was designed for a person who did not
exist: someone with no children, no bad nights, and no mornings that
started with a phone already buzzing.

A calmer routine starts from the opposite assumption. Assume the hard
mornings. Assume you slept badly, the house is cold, and something is
already going wrong. Build for that morning, and the good mornings take
care of themselves.

## Three anchors, not a schedule

A schedule breaks the moment one thing slips. An anchor is different: it
is a single action you return to no matter what time it is. I keep three.

**Light first.** Before the phone, I open a window or step outside for
one minute. Not for a wellness reason I can cite — just because looking
at the sky instead of a screen changes the tone of the next hour. On the
worst mornings this is the only anchor I hit, and it is enough.

**One warm thing, made slowly.** Coffee, usually. The point is not the
coffee. The point is the four minutes where I am doing one thing with my
hands and nothing else is allowed in. No podcast, no inbox. It is the
smallest possible meditation, disguised as a chore I was going to do
anyway.

**One sentence, written down.** Not a journal. A journal is a project,
and projects fail. One sentence: what is the single thing that would make
today feel handled? Some mornings it is "reply to the landlord." Some
mornings it is "go for a walk." Writing it turns a vague pressure into a
task I can actually finish.

That is the whole routine. Light, a warm thing, a sentence. On a good
morning it expands on its own — the sentence becomes a plan, the coffee
becomes breakfast with someone. On a bad morning it shrinks to sixty
seconds at a window. Either way it holds.

## Why the small version wins

The elaborate routines all shared one flaw: they were fragile. Each new
habit multiplied the number of ways the morning could fail. Miss one and
the whole sequence felt broken, so you skipped the rest too. A three-
anchor routine has almost nothing to break. There is no streak to lose,
no app to guilt you, no optimal window you missed by sleeping in.

There is also a quieter benefit. When the routine is small, it stops
being the point. The goal was never to have an impressive morning — it
was to start the day feeling like a person instead of a queue of
notifications. Once the routine takes four minutes instead of ninety, it
gets out of the way and lets the actual day begin.

## Making it stick

If you want to try this, resist the urge to add. The instinct after a few
good days is to bolt on a workout, a supplement, a second journal. Don't.
The moment the routine needs a checklist, it has become the thing you are
managing rather than the thing that steadies you.

Keep it embarrassingly small for a month. Let the mornings prove that the
small version is enough. If, after that, one more anchor genuinely earns
its place — a short walk, ten pushups, a page of a book — add exactly one
and watch it for another month.

The best morning routine is the one you still do on the morning you feel
least like doing anything. Everything else is decoration.
